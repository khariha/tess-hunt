# Candidate briefing (talking points)

Status language: these are **planet candidates**, not planets, until promoted/validated. Note AAS82771 under review at RNAAS.

## TIC 389723320 — the clean one
- **Sky:** constellation Grus (the Crane), 3 degrees from Alnair, the crane's brightest star. Grus sits below Piscis Austrinus; find Fomalhaut, drop south.
- **Distance:** 126 pc = 412 light-years. The light TESS recorded in 2018 left the star around 1606, three years before Galileo first pointed a telescope at the sky.
- **Seeing it:** 16th magnitude, never by eye. From Orlando the field reaches 17 degrees above the southern horizon on autumn evenings; from San Francisco about 8 degrees, so effectively on the horizon.
- **Star:** red dwarf, 3,270 K (the Sun is 5,800), 0.37 solar radii and masses, about 1.4 percent of the Sun's light. Deep orange-red.
- **Planet:** 4.6 Earth radii, 1.2 times Neptune. Orbits every 3.927 days (94 hours) at 0.029 AU, 4.3 million km, 16.6 star radii. Moves at 80 km/s. The star would span 7 degrees in its sky, 13 times the Sun's apparent size. Receives 17 times Earth's sunlight; equilibrium temperature about 565 K / 290 C / 560 F.
- **Type:** hot sub-Neptune; closest known twin is GJ 3470 b.
- **Evidence:** 36 transits across 6 sectors spanning 2018 to 2026; dip on the target pixel at 14 to 18 sigma; period known to 0.1 second.

## TIC 114949540 — the hot Neptune
- **Sky:** constellation Microscopium, a faint 18th-century constellation between Grus and Sagittarius; nearest bright stars are gamma Gruis and alpha Indi, each about 11 degrees away.
- **Distance:** 168 pc = 549 light-years. Light left around 1477, before Columbus sailed.
- **Seeing it:** 17th magnitude. From Orlando 24 degrees up in the south on autumn evenings; from SF about 15.
- **Star:** red dwarf, 3,320 K, 0.42 solar radii and masses, about 2 percent of the Sun's light.
- **Planet:** 4.35 Earth radii, 1.1 times Neptune. Year of 2.739 days (66 hours) at 0.026 AU, 3.8 million km, 13 star radii, 102 km/s. Star spans 9 degrees in its sky, 16 times the Sun's. 29 times Earth's irradiation; about 670 K / 400 C / 745 F.
- **Type:** hot Neptune; closest twin is GJ 436 b, the first Neptune ever seen to transit (2007).
- **Evidence:** 25 transits over 3 sectors; on target at 15 sigma.

## TIC 259719966 — the Jupiter-sized one
- **Sky:** constellation Indus (the Indian), deep southern sky, between Tucana and Pavo. Nearest bright stars alpha Tucanae and beta Indi, 6 to 7 degrees. The Small Magellanic Cloud, a companion galaxy to the Milky Way, is about 17 degrees away.
- **Distance:** 147 pc = 480 light-years. Light left around 1546, three years after Copernicus published.
- **Seeing it:** never rises from Orlando or San Francisco (declination -64). From Key West it grazes the horizon; from Hawaii, Australia, Chile it is easy.
- **Star:** red dwarf, 3,440 K, 0.49 solar radii and masses, about 3 percent of the Sun's light. Tmag 13.8, the brightest of the three hosts.
- **Companion:** 0.94 Jupiter radii (10.6 Earth radii, 67,000 km). Year of 11.077 days at about 0.086 AU, 13 million km, 38 star radii, 84 km/s. Star spans 3 degrees in its sky, 6 times the Sun's. About 4 times Earth's irradiation; equilibrium temperature about 396 K / 123 C / 253 F, a warm world rather than a scorched one.
- **Nature:** radius alone cannot separate a giant planet, a brown dwarf, or a star at the hydrogen-burning limit; all are about Jupiter-sized. A spectrograph mass measurement decides. Giant companions to red dwarfs are rare and theoretically awkward, so whichever it is, it is interesting.
- **Evidence:** 20 transits over 9 sectors; dip on target at 33 and 37 sigma in two 200-second sectors; odd/even equal, no secondary eclipse.

## One-liners
